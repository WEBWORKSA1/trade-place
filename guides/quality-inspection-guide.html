Quality Inspection for Imports: From Pre-Production to Pre-Shipment

Plan import quality control step by step: pre-production, in-line and pre-shipment inspections, AQL sampling in plain English, golden samples and lab testing.

7 min read Updated October 2026 Trade.Place Editorial Team
Key takeaways
  • Inspections are cheapest and most useful when they happen before you pay the balance, not after the goods arrive.
  • AQL sampling lets you check a statistically chosen sample instead of every unit, using limits you agree with the supplier in advance.
  • A signed golden sample and a written specification are the reference points every inspection depends on.
  • Inspection checks workmanship and conformity; lab testing checks safety and regulatory compliance. You often need both.

When you buy from a factory thousands of kilometres away, the first time you see the goods might be when you open the container. By then, fixing problems is slow and expensive. A planned quality control process moves those checks earlier, when the factory can still rework, replace or repack. This guide walks through the main inspection types, explains AQL sampling without the jargon, and shows how to build a checklist that inspectors can actually use.

The main types of inspection

Inspections are usually carried out by an independent third-party inspection company, your own QC staff, or a sourcing agent. Each type has a different purpose.

InspectionWhenMain purpose
Factory auditBefore placing the first orderConfirms the supplier exists, has the equipment and capacity claimed, and runs basic quality systems. Social and environmental audits are separate types.
Pre-production inspectionBefore mass production startsChecks raw materials, components and the first articles against the specification.
During production inspectionCommonly when a portion of the order is completeCatches defects early so the factory can correct the process for the rest of the run.
Pre-shipment inspectionWhen production is complete and most goods are packedRandom sampling of finished goods for workmanship, function, measurements, labelling and packaging.
Container loading checkDuring loadingConfirms quantity, carton condition, correct goods and proper loading, then records the seal number.

For a first order with a new supplier, many importers combine a factory audit with a pre-shipment inspection. For complex, high-value or long production runs, a during-production check adds valuable warning time.

AQL sampling in plain terms

Checking every unit in a shipment of thousands is rarely practical. Instead, inspectors pull a random sample and decide whether the whole lot passes based on how many defects they find. The method most commonly used is based on the ISO 2859-1 standard, published by the International Organization for Standardization. In trade it is usually called AQL, short for acceptance quality limit.

The three settings you choose

  1. Inspection level. This determines how large the sample is relative to the lot size. General Inspection Level II is the usual default. Level I uses a smaller sample; Level III uses a larger one. Special levels with very small samples exist for tests that are slow or destructive.
  2. Defect categories. Defects are typically graded as critical (unsafe or illegal), major (likely to cause a return or make the product unsellable) and minor (a flaw that is unlikely to cause a return).
  3. AQL value for each category. A lower number is stricter. Common choices for consumer goods are 2.5 for major defects and 4.0 for minor defects, with critical defects often set to zero tolerance. Your product, market and customer expectations may justify stricter or looser limits.

How the decision works

The AQL tables give the inspector, for a given lot size and inspection level, a sample size and an accept number for each defect class. If the number of defects found is at or below the accept number, that class passes. If it exceeds it, the lot fails for that class.

Example

Suppose the tables tell your inspector to check a sample of a few hundred units from your lot, with an accept number of a handful of major defects. If they find fewer majors than that limit, the shipment passes on majors. If they find more, the report shows a fail and you decide what happens next. Your inspection company will look up the exact figures for your lot size.

Remember that AQL is a sampling plan, not a guarantee. A shipment that passes may still contain some defective units. Agree the AQL levels with your supplier in writing before production starts, so a failed result is not a surprise to either side.

The golden sample and the specification

An inspector can only judge goods against something. That reference has two parts:

  • The golden sample is an approved production-standard sample, signed and sealed by both buyer and supplier. Keep one with the factory and one with you, and send photos to the inspector. If the sample was handmade or modified, request a new one from actual production tooling.
  • The written specification lists materials, dimensions with tolerances, colours (ideally referenced to a colour standard), finishes, weights, functions, labels, markings, packaging and carton marks.

Disagreements usually come from gaps between the two, such as an approved sample in a slightly different shade than the colour written in the spec. Resolve these before mass production.

Building a practical QC checklist

A good checklist turns your specification into checks an inspector can perform on the factory floor. Typical sections include:

  1. Quantity: number of finished and packed units versus the order.
  2. Packaging and marking: carton dimensions and weight, shipping marks, barcodes, retail packaging, inserts, labels and warnings.
  3. Visual workmanship: scratches, stains, loose threads, uneven seams, burrs, misprints, each mapped to critical, major or minor.
  4. Measurements: key dimensions and weight against tolerances.
  5. Function and safety: on-site tests such as assembly, operation, drop test of cartons, simple electrical safety checks or fit tests.
  6. Comparison with golden sample: colour, texture, logo position and overall appearance.

Include photos of acceptable and unacceptable examples where you can. The clearer the checklist, the more consistent the result between different inspectors. Packaging data from the inspection, such as actual carton sizes, is also worth feeding back into your container load plan.

Lab testing and compliance marks

On-site inspection does not prove a product is legal to sell. Many products need laboratory testing against the regulations of the destination market: for example chemical content limits for toys and textiles, electrical safety and electromagnetic compatibility for electronics, or food-contact rules for kitchenware.

Compliance marks and declarations vary by market. Examples include CE marking for many product categories in the European Union, UKCA marking in Great Britain, and FCC requirements for many electronic devices in the United States. Responsibility for compliance often sits with the importer or the party placing the product on the market, not only the factory. Check the official rules for your product and market, for example through the European Commission or GOV.UK, and confirm which tests apply with an accredited lab.

Verify test reports

A supplier's existing test report may cover a different model, an older version, or another company. Check the report details directly with the issuing lab before relying on it.

Who pays, and what happens after the report

In most small-business imports the buyer books and pays for inspections, which keeps the inspector working for the buyer. Some buyers negotiate that the supplier pays for re-inspections after a failure. Write this into the purchase order.

A typical report gives an overall result of pass, fail or pending (a judgement call for the buyer). After a fail, common outcomes are:

  • The factory sorts and reworks the goods, followed by a re-inspection.
  • The buyer accepts the goods with a price adjustment, if the defects are cosmetic.
  • Production is partly or fully remade for serious failures.

Frequently asked questions

What AQL levels should I use?

For general consumer goods, many buyers use General Inspection Level II with an AQL of 2.5 for major defects and 4.0 for minor defects, and zero tolerance for critical defects. Higher-risk or premium products often justify stricter settings.

When should a pre-shipment inspection happen?

It is normally done when production is complete and most of the goods are packed, so the inspector can sample from finished cartons. It should take place before you pay the balance.

Does a passed inspection mean every unit is perfect?

No. AQL sampling accepts a small number of defects within agreed limits. It reduces risk significantly but cannot guarantee that every unit is free of defects.

Is a factory audit the same as an inspection?

No. An audit evaluates the supplier's capabilities and systems, while an inspection evaluates a specific batch of products.

Do I still need lab testing if the goods pass inspection?

Often yes. Inspection checks workmanship and conformity to your specification, while lab testing checks safety and regulatory requirements that cannot be verified by eye on the factory floor.
