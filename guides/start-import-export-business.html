How to Start an Import-Export Business: A Practical Roadmap

Start an import-export business step by step: choose a model and niche, register, open banking, find suppliers and buyers, price for profit and plan your first 90 days.

6 min read Updated October 2026 Trade.Place Editorial Team
Key takeaways
  • Pick a business model first. An importer-distributor, a sourcing agent and an e-commerce seller need very different capital and skills.
  • Choose a niche where you have an edge: product knowledge, customer access or supplier access.
  • Most countries require a trader identification number for customs, alongside normal business registration.
  • Price on full landed cost and protect your margin from currency swings, returns and delays.

Import-export is one of the most accessible ways to build a trading business. You do not need a factory, and a single well-chosen product can be enough to start. But the businesses that last are rarely built on a lucky product find. They are built on a clear model, a focused niche, sound paperwork and careful margins. This roadmap takes you from idea to first shipments, with a 90-day plan at the end.

Choose your business model

ModelHow you earnCapital neededMain risk
Importer / distributorBuy, import and resell to retailers or businesses at a markupHigher: stock, freight, duties, warehousingUnsold inventory and cash tied up
Export agentCommission from producers for finding overseas buyersLowBeing cut out once parties meet
Sourcing agentFees or commission from buyers for finding and managing suppliersLow to mediumLiability if quality fails
E-commerce importerImport products and sell direct to consumers onlineMediumMarketing costs and platform dependence
Merchant traderBuy and resell goods between countries, often without the goods passing through your own countryMedium to highPayment and documentation complexity

Agency models let you start with little capital but require strong networks and written commission agreements. Stock-holding models offer more control and margin but need working capital and logistics skills.

Select a niche you can win in

A good niche sits where three things overlap: demand you can reach, supply you can access and an edge you can explain. Questions to test an idea:

  • Who exactly will buy? Name the customer type, for example independent garden centres or restaurant suppliers, not "everyone."
  • What is your edge? Industry experience, language skills, contacts in a producing country or a better product version.
  • Is it manageable? Fragile, perishable, highly regulated or very heavy goods add cost and complexity for a first venture.
  • Do the numbers work? Estimate landed cost and achievable selling price before falling in love with a product.
  • Can you repeat it? Consumables and replacement items build repeat orders; one-off novelties do not.
Example

A former chef who knows commercial kitchens might import a focused range of specialised kitchen tools for small restaurants. Their edge is knowing which products cooks actually want and how to reach the buyers.

Register the business and get trader numbers

Requirements differ by country, so treat this as a checklist of things to ask your local authorities and advisers about rather than a definitive list.

  1. Business structure. Choose between a sole trader, partnership or limited company. Liability protection matters when you hold stock and sign supply contracts.
  2. Tax registration. Register for income or corporate tax and, where applicable, sales tax, VAT or GST. Import VAT treatment can significantly affect cash flow.
  3. Customs identification. Many countries require a trader number before you can import or export. In the UK and EU this is an EORI number. In India, exporters and importers generally need an Importer-Exporter Code (IEC). In the United States, importers are identified to U.S. Customs and Border Protection by an importer number, commonly linked to a tax identification number. Check your own customs authority's website for the current process.
  4. Licences and product rules. Some products need specific permits, registrations or labelling. Check controls on both the export and import side.
  5. Insurance. Consider cargo insurance, product liability and general business cover.

Set up banking and manage currency

Open a business bank account that handles international transfers smoothly. Ask your bank about multi-currency accounts, transfer charges, letters of credit and tools for fixing exchange rates in advance. If you buy in one currency and sell in another, build an FX buffer into your pricing and keep the time between paying suppliers and receiving customer payments as short as you can. Our payment terms guide covers the main options, and the currency converter helps with quick estimates.

Find suppliers and customers

Suppliers

  • Online B2B marketplaces and directories for initial discovery.
  • Trade shows, which let you meet many manufacturers in person; see our trade show directory.
  • Trade promotion agencies and chambers of commerce in producing countries.
  • Structured RFQs: post a buying request to collect comparable quotes.

Always verify before paying. Read how to find and verify suppliers and the guide to avoiding supplier scams.

Customers

  • Direct outreach to retailers, wholesalers or business buyers in your niche, with samples ready.
  • Your own website and online marketplaces for consumer sales.
  • Trade shows in your selling market, as a visitor or exhibitor.
  • Government export support services, which in many countries offer market research and introductions; in the US, for example, see trade.gov.

Price for profit

Start from your full landed cost: product price, freight, insurance, duties, taxes that you cannot reclaim, clearance and inland delivery. The landed cost calculator pulls these together. Then add your own operating costs: warehousing, packaging, marketing, platform fees, returns, payment fees and your time.

Understand the difference between markup (profit as a share of cost) and margin (profit as a share of selling price). Use the profit margin calculator to test prices. If wholesale customers need room for their own margin, check that your price still works at their expected retail level. If the margin only works when everything goes perfectly, the product is too risky for a first order.

Build your logistics and compliance team

Few small traders handle logistics alone. Core partners typically include:

  • Freight forwarder to book transport and coordinate the shipment. Compare options with a freight quote request.
  • Customs broker to classify goods, file entries and advise on duties.
  • Inspection provider to check quality before you pay the balance.
  • Warehouse or fulfilment partner if you do not want to store goods yourself.
  • Accountant familiar with import VAT, duties and foreign-currency transactions.

Learn the basics yourself even when you outsource: Incoterms, HS classification and customs documentation. You remain responsible as the importer of record. Our guides on customs clearance and how to import products are good starting points.

Your first 90 days

  1. Days 1–30: validate. Choose your model and niche, speak to potential customers, estimate landed cost for two or three candidate products and register the business and trader numbers.
  2. Days 31–60: source. Send RFQs, order and compare samples, verify your top suppliers, open banking and line up a forwarder and broker.
  3. Days 61–90: execute. Place a modest first order, book a pre-shipment inspection, track documents and clearance, and secure early customers or pre-orders. Review costs against your estimates once the goods land.
Start small

A smaller first order costs more per unit but limits the damage if a product, supplier or market assumption turns out to be wrong. Many successful traders treat the first shipment as paid market research.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need to start an import-export business?

It depends heavily on the model. Agency and sourcing models can start with very little capital, while importing and holding stock requires money for goods, freight, duties and storage before you are paid by customers.

Do I need a licence to import or export?

Most countries require a trader or customs identification number, such as an EORI number in the UK and EU or an IEC in India. Specific products may also need permits. Check with your customs authority and relevant regulators.

Can I run an import-export business from home?

Yes, many small traders do, especially with a forwarder and a warehouse or fulfilment partner handling the physical goods. Check any local rules on registering a business at a home address.

What is the difference between an importer and a sourcing agent?

An importer buys goods and takes ownership and risk. A sourcing agent finds and manages suppliers for clients, usually for a fee or commission, without owning the goods.

How do I choose my first product?

Choose a product you understand, with clear demand from a customer group you can reach, manageable compliance requirements and a margin that still works after the full landed cost.
