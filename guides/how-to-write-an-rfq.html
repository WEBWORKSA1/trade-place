How to Write an RFQ That Gets Accurate Supplier Quotes

Write a request for quotation that suppliers can price accurately: essential fields, a ready-to-use RFQ template, and how to compare quotes like for like.

6 min read Updated October 2026 Trade.Place Editorial Team
Key takeaways
  • A vague RFQ gets vague prices. Suppliers can only price what you define.
  • Always state the Incoterm and named place, quantity tiers, packaging and required certifications.
  • Use one template for every supplier so the quotes come back in a comparable format.
  • Compare quotes on landed cost, not unit price, before you negotiate.

A request for quotation, or RFQ, is the message that starts most sourcing deals. Done well, it brings back quotes you can compare line by line and act on with confidence. Done badly, it brings back prices for slightly different products, under different shipping terms, at different quantities, so none of them can be compared. This guide explains what to include, gives you a template, and shows how to turn a pile of quotes into a sound decision.

RFQ, RFI and RFP: what is the difference?

  • RFI (request for information) asks suppliers about their capabilities, such as products, capacity, certifications and markets. Use it when you are still exploring.
  • RFQ (request for quotation) asks for prices for a clearly defined product or service. Use it when you know what you want.
  • RFP (request for proposal) asks suppliers to propose a solution and approach, not just a price. Use it for complex projects where the method matters, such as a custom-engineered product or an outsourced service.

Most small importers buying physical goods need an RFQ. If you are not yet sure what specification to buy, a short RFI round or sample review first will make your RFQ much sharper.

The essential fields

Product definition

  • Specification: product name, function and model reference, ideally with drawings or reference photos.
  • Materials: grade, composition, thickness or weight, for example a specific steel grade or fabric weight.
  • Dimensions and tolerances: key measurements and how much variation is acceptable.
  • Finish and colour: surface treatment, colour references, printing or logo requirements.

Commercial terms

  • Quantity tiers: ask for prices at two or three quantities, for example your first order and a likely repeat volume. This shows how pricing scales and where the supplier's real minimum lies.
  • Target price: optional. Sharing a realistic target can save time; sharing an unrealistic one can lead to quietly downgraded quality. Many buyers share a range rather than a number.
  • Incoterm and named place: for example FOB with a named port of loading, or DAP with your warehouse address. Without this, quotes cannot be compared. Our Incoterms 2020 tool explains each term.
  • Destination: country and city, even if you ask for FOB, so suppliers can flag export or compliance issues.
  • Payment terms: your preferred method and structure, such as a deposit and balance against inspection.
  • Quote validity: ask how long the price holds.

Packaging, compliance and timing

  • Packaging: retail packaging, inner packs, carton size limits, labels, barcodes and shipping marks.
  • Certifications and testing: required marks or standards for your market and who pays for testing.
  • Timeline: sample deadline, required ship date, and request for production lead time.
  • Samples: how many, whether paid, and whether the cost is refundable against a first order.
  • Quality: your intended inspection approach and AQL levels, as explained in the quality inspection guide.

How to compare quotes like for like

Quotes almost never arrive in identical form. Before comparing, normalise them:

  1. Same product. Check each quote against your specification. Flag any substitution of materials, sizes or packaging.
  2. Same quantity. Compare prices at the same tier.
  3. Same Incoterm. Convert each quote to a common delivery point by adding freight, insurance and local charges where needed.
  4. Same currency. Convert at a consistent rate.
  5. One-off costs. Spread tooling, moulds, setup or plate charges across the order quantity.
  6. Landed cost. Add duties, taxes, clearance and inland delivery. The landed cost calculator does this quickly, and the landed cost guide explains each element.

Then score the non-price factors: responsiveness, quality of answers, samples, certifications, lead time and payment terms. A slightly higher price from a supplier who answered every question clearly is often the better deal.

Negotiating after the quotes arrive

  • Negotiate on more than price. Payment terms, lead time, free samples, included packaging or a reduced MOQ can be worth as much as a discount.
  • Use real alternatives. Mentioning that you have competitive quotes is fair. Inventing them tends to backfire.
  • Ask what drives the price. Often a small specification change, such as a stock colour or standard carton, reduces cost without hurting the product.
  • Protect quality. If a supplier drops their price sharply, ask what changed and confirm the specification in writing.
  • Check your margin. Run your target selling price through the profit margin calculator so you know your walk-away point.

MOQ tips for small buyers

Minimum order quantities exist because of setup costs, material minimums and production efficiency. To work within them:

  • Ask whether the MOQ applies per colour, per size or per order. Combining variants can help.
  • Choose stock materials and colours rather than custom ones.
  • Offer a slightly higher unit price for a lower first order, with a commitment to standard pricing on repeat orders.
  • Consider trading companies, which may combine your order with others.

Frequently asked questions

How many suppliers should I send an RFQ to?

Many buyers aim for a handful of qualified suppliers, enough to see the market range without being overwhelmed. Quality of the shortlist matters more than the number.

Should I include a target price?

It can speed things up if your target is realistic. If it is too low, suppliers may cut quality to meet it. A price range is a common compromise.

Why do I need to state an Incoterm?

The Incoterm defines which costs and risks are included in the price. Without it, one supplier may quote ex-works and another delivered, and the prices cannot be compared.

What if suppliers ignore parts of my RFQ?

Ask them to complete the missing fields. A supplier who will not answer basic questions before an order is unlikely to communicate better after you pay.

How long should I give suppliers to respond?

For a standard product, a week or so is usually reasonable. Custom or engineered items may need longer, especially if the supplier must price tooling.
