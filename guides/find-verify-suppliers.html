Sourcing

How to Find and Verify Reliable Overseas Suppliers

Where to find overseas suppliers and how to verify them: licences, factory vs trader checks, audits, references, samples, video tours and third-party checks.

6 min read Updated October 2026 Trade.Place Editorial Team
Key takeaways
  • Use more than one channel to find suppliers: marketplaces, trade shows, agents and directories each surface different businesses.
  • Verification is about evidence, not impressions. Check registration documents, the bank account name and the factory itself.
  • Knowing whether you are dealing with a factory or a trading company changes how you negotiate and what you check.
  • Samples and a small first order are part of verification, not a formality.

A good supplier is the foundation of a profitable import business. A poor one costs you far more than the price difference you saved: late deliveries, failed products, returns, damaged reviews and, in the worst cases, money paid for goods that never ship. This guide covers where to look and how to separate capable, honest suppliers from the rest.

Where to find overseas suppliers

B2B marketplaces

Large B2B marketplaces such as Alibaba.com, Global Sources or Made-in-China.com list a huge number of manufacturers and trading companies. They are fast for research: you can compare product ranges, minimum order quantities and indicative prices in an afternoon. The trade-off is noise. Listings are often written by sales teams, photos may be shared between sellers, and “verified” badges vary in what they actually check. Treat marketplace profiles as a lead, not as proof.

Trade shows

Meeting suppliers face to face at an industry fair lets you handle products, judge a team’s technical knowledge and compare dozens of exhibitors in a few days. Exhibiting costs money, which filters out some of the least serious sellers. Browse our trade show calendar and read the trade shows guide to plan a productive visit.

Sourcing agents

A sourcing agent based in the supplier’s country can find factories, negotiate in the local language, follow production and arrange inspections. Good agents save time and reduce risk; weak ones add a hidden margin or steer you to factories that pay them commission. Agree in writing how the agent is paid, and ask whether they receive anything from suppliers.

Directories, associations and referrals

Industry associations, chambers of commerce, export promotion agencies and government trade portals often maintain member or exporter lists. Customs import records, offered by some data providers, can show which factories already ship to companies in your market. Referrals from non-competing importers are among the most reliable leads of all.

Let suppliers come to you

A clear, detailed request attracts better replies than a vague one. Write a proper specification using our RFQ guide, then post a buying request to reach suppliers that match.

Shortlisting: the first filter

Send the same request to eight to fifteen potential suppliers and judge the replies, not just the prices. Strong suppliers ask questions about your specification, explain what drives cost, quote in a clear structure and give realistic lead times. Weak ones reply with a generic price list, agree to everything instantly or quote far below everyone else. An unusually low price is a warning sign, not a bargain: it usually means a different material, a thinner gauge, missing certification or an intention to renegotiate after you pay.

Factory or trading company?

Both can be good partners. A factory may offer lower prices and direct control over production. A trading company may offer a wider range, better English, consolidation of mixed products and smaller minimums. The problem is not which one you use; it is being misled about which one you are dealing with.

Clues that help you tell them apart:

  • The registered business scope on the licence mentions manufacturing or production, not only trading or sales.
  • The product range is focused on one category rather than dozens of unrelated items.
  • Staff can discuss machinery, materials, tooling and process capacity in detail.
  • The address on the licence matches the factory you see on a video call or visit.

Third-party verification and factory audits

For larger orders, independent inspection and audit firms can visit the factory, confirm it exists, check its legal status and assess capacity, quality systems and working conditions. A basic verification visit costs relatively little compared with a container of goods, and the written report gives you a baseline to compare against later. See our quality inspection guide for how audits and inspections differ.

Samples: test like a customer would

Order samples from your top two or three suppliers at the same time. Test them as your customer would use them, and against any regulations that apply in your market. Measure dimensions, weigh components, check colour against a reference and, where needed, send a sample to an accredited lab. Write down every change you request so the production version can be checked against an agreed standard.

Start small and build the relationship

Even after every check, a first order is still a test. Keep it modest, inspect before shipment and pay the balance only when the inspection passes. Track performance on quality, communication, lead times and how the supplier handles problems. A supplier who admits a mistake and fixes it is often more valuable than one whose first order happened to go perfectly. When you calculate whether a supplier is worth it, include defect rates and delays, not just unit price — the profit margin calculator helps you see the real effect.

Bank details changed? Stop.

A message saying the supplier’s bank account has changed is one of the most common fraud patterns in trade. Confirm any change by phone or video using contact details you already had, never those in the new message. Read how to avoid supplier scams for other warning signs.

Frequently asked questions

Is a “verified” badge on a marketplace enough?

No. Badges usually confirm that certain documents were checked or that an inspection company visited at some point. They do not guarantee product quality, current ownership of the factory or that the person messaging you works for that company. Use them as one input among many.

How many suppliers should I contact?

Contacting eight to fifteen with an identical specification is a practical range for most products. It gives you enough replies to compare prices and responsiveness, and enough to narrow down to two or three for sampling.

Is it a problem if my supplier is a trading company?

Not necessarily. Many trading companies offer real value through consolidation, quality control and communication. The issue arises when a trader claims to be a factory, because you cannot then judge their margin, control or capacity accurately.

Do I need to visit the factory in person?

It helps, especially for large or long-term orders, but it is not always practical. A live video tour combined with a third-party verification visit and independent pre-shipment inspection covers much of the same ground for a small business.

Should I pay for samples?

Yes, in most cases. Paying a fair price, plus courier costs, signals that you are a serious buyer and gives you the right to insist the samples match your specification. Many suppliers credit sample costs against the first bulk order.
