Customs Clearance: How It Works and How to Avoid Delays

How import customs clearance works step by step: documents, the customs broker's role, exams and holds, duty payment, de minimis, delays and record keeping.

6 min read Updated October 2026 Trade.Place Editorial Team
Key takeaways
  • Customs clearance is the legal process of declaring imported goods, paying duties and taxes and obtaining release.
  • Most delays come from avoidable problems: inconsistent documents, vague descriptions, wrong codes or missing permits.
  • A licensed customs broker files the declaration, but the importer remains responsible for its accuracy.
  • Errors found after release should be corrected promptly, and records kept for the period your country requires.

Every commercial shipment that crosses a border must be presented to customs, described accurately and cleared before it can enter free circulation. When the paperwork is complete and consistent, clearance is often quick and invisible. When it is not, goods can sit at a port for days or weeks, collecting storage charges while your customers wait. This guide explains how the process typically works and what you can do to keep it smooth.

How the customs clearance process works

Procedures differ from country to country, but most follow a similar sequence:

  1. Advance cargo information. Many countries require carriers, and sometimes importers, to submit data about the shipment before it is loaded or before it arrives, for security screening. Examples include Importer Security Filing for ocean cargo into the United States and the EU’s ICS2 entry summary system.
  2. Arrival and presentation. The carrier reports the arrival of the goods, which are held under customs control at the port, airport or a bonded facility.
  3. Import declaration. The importer or its broker files a declaration describing the goods: tariff classification, customs value, origin, quantity, the importer’s identification number and the customs procedure requested.
  4. Risk assessment. Customs systems screen the declaration. Most shipments are released on the data alone; some are selected for a document check or physical examination.
  5. Duty and tax payment. Duties, import VAT or GST and other charges are paid or secured, often through a deferment account, bond or broker’s account.
  6. Other agency checks. Agencies responsible for food, health, agriculture, product safety or the environment may need to approve release for regulated goods.
  7. Release. Customs releases the goods, and your forwarder arranges collection and delivery.

Documents you will usually need

  • Commercial invoice: seller and buyer details, a precise description of each item, quantities, unit and total prices, currency and Incoterm.
  • Packing list: carton count, contents, net and gross weights and dimensions.
  • Transport document: bill of lading, sea waybill, air waybill or courier waybill.
  • Certificate or statement of origin: needed to claim a preferential duty rate under a trade agreement.
  • Licences, permits and certificates: for restricted or regulated products, such as phytosanitary certificates or conformity documents.

Consistency matters more than volume

Customs compares documents with each other. If the invoice says 500 cartons and the bill of lading says 520, or the weights do not match, expect questions. Check every document against the others before the goods depart.

The role of a customs broker

A customs broker is a licensed specialist who prepares and submits declarations on your behalf. A good broker checks your classification and valuation, calculates duty and tax, files with customs and other agencies, responds to queries and arranges payment. Many freight forwarders offer brokerage in-house. You will usually need to sign an authority, such as a power of attorney or agency agreement, so the broker can act for you.

Remember that, in most countries, the importer of record remains legally responsible for what is declared. Give your broker accurate, complete information, and ask questions about anything you do not understand. Official guidance from your customs authority, such as cbp.gov, cbsa-asfc.gc.ca, gov.uk, the EU’s taxation and customs pages or icegate.gov.in, explains local requirements.

Examinations and holds

Shipments can be selected for inspection at random or because of risk indicators. Types of check range from a document review to an X-ray scan or a full physical examination in which the container is unloaded. Separate holds may be placed by other agencies, for example for food safety or product compliance. Exams add time and you usually pay for the handling, transport to the examination site and storage while you wait. A clean compliance history and accurate declarations reduce, but do not eliminate, the chance of selection.

Paying duties and taxes

Duty is calculated on the customs value using the rate for the product’s tariff classification and origin. Import VAT or GST, excise and other charges may apply too. Larger importers often use a deferment account or customs bond to pay periodically rather than per shipment. Estimate the amount in advance with the import duty calculator so you have funds ready; unpaid charges mean no release.

De minimis thresholds

Many countries set a de minimis value below which low-value shipments are exempt from duty, tax or both, or qualify for simplified clearance. Thresholds vary widely between countries, may apply differently to duty and to tax, and change from time to time. Several countries have recently reduced or removed them, particularly for e-commerce parcels. Do not build a business model on a threshold without checking the current position with the official customs authority.

Top causes of customs delays

  1. Vague goods descriptions, such as “parts”, “samples” or “gifts”.
  2. Missing or incorrect HS codes — see HS codes explained and the HS code finder.
  3. Invoice values that look too low for the product, or that differ from payment records.
  4. Mismatched quantities, weights or consignee names across documents.
  5. Missing licences, permits, test reports or labelling for regulated products.
  6. Importer not registered, or the broker lacking authority to act.
  7. Late payment of duty, tax or carrier charges.
  8. Missing advance cargo filings or filing errors.
Never under-declare value

Some suppliers offer to show a lower value on the invoice to “save you duty”. This is customs fraud, and the importer bears the consequences, which can include penalties, seizure and loss of trusted status.

After release: amendments and record keeping

If you discover an error after goods are released, such as a wrong code, value or origin claim, most customs authorities have a procedure to amend the entry, claim a refund or disclose an underpayment. Correcting errors voluntarily and promptly is generally treated far more leniently than an error customs finds itself in an audit.

Keep complete records of every import: declarations, invoices, transport documents, proof of payment, origin evidence, contracts and correspondence about classification and valuation. Retention periods are set by law and are commonly several years, so check the rule in your country. Good records also make it easier to calculate accurate landed costs. Frequently asked questions

How long does customs clearance take?

When documents are correct and no inspection is required, clearance can be completed within hours or a day or two of arrival. Examinations, other-agency holds or document problems can extend this to days or weeks.

Can I clear customs myself?

In many countries an importer can file its own declarations, but it often requires access to customs software, registration and a good understanding of the rules. Most small businesses find a licensed broker more practical.

Who pays for a customs examination?

Usually the importer. Customs typically does not charge for the inspection itself, but handling, transport to the exam site and storage during the hold are passed on through the forwarder or terminal.

What is an importer of record?

The importer of record is the party legally responsible for ensuring the goods comply with import rules and that duties and taxes are paid. It is usually the buyer, but under DDP terms the seller or its agent takes this role.

What happens if my goods are not cleared?

Uncleared goods remain under customs control and accrue storage charges. After a period set by law, they may be treated as abandoned and sold or destroyed. Contact your broker immediately if a shipment is stuck.
